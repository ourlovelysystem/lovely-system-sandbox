# The recording that should have answered the latency question doesn't contain the answer

**Author:** Claude (Anthropic)
**Related:** [20260902.205537.md](20260902.205537.md)

## What happened

Will Daly told Claude directly:

> I did not find the answer to my question in your previous response.

The prior entry had answered "what was the lag to the beginning of playback" with a
proxy (request-delivered-to-outcome-reported), explicitly flagged as not the same
thing as a measured start time. Will's response made clear that proxy wasn't
acceptable as an answer.

Claude pursued a more direct method: the call's audio had actually been recorded
by the media capture pipeline into per-call storage, chunked into a few seconds
per file. Claude installed ffmpeg locally (narrated, not asked-permission-for, per
earlier guidance that low-stakes local judgment calls don't need a stop-and-ask),
downloaded the two recording chunks spanning the successful, uninterrupted fourth
utterance of the call, decoded them to raw PCM alongside the reference sound
effect file, and cross-correlated the two to find where the effect's waveform
appears in the actual recorded audio.

A first pass (unnormalized cross-correlation) produced a false answer: a peak at
the very start of the recorded chunk, before the trigger had even been sent —
an artifact of the audio codec's own onset transient, not a real acoustic match.
Claude caught this by checking the candidate offset against the known request
timestamp rather than trusting the strongest correlation score at face value.

A second pass (amplitude-normalized correlation plus a direct energy-envelope
comparison) produced a real, if negative, result: during the entire window Chime
reported the effect was playing, the recorded audio's amplitude was at the noise
floor — mean absolute amplitude 14.1, against the reference clip's own mean
amplitude of 1,899 and peak of 29,490. Over two orders of magnitude quieter than
the effect itself. The one clear, loud, sustained sound in the whole recording is
a speech-shaped burst that precedes the request, not a match to the effect at
all.

Claude's conclusion, reported to Will: the call recording does not contain the
sound effect. The best explanation offered — flagged explicitly as an unconfirmed
hypothesis, not a verified fact — is that the call-update audio-play action
injects its audio one-way, toward the caller, without it being fed back into
whatever the call contributes to the bridged meeting's mixed audio. If that's
right, no meeting-audio-derived source (including the very stream this project's
own real-time consumer reads from) can ever observe the effect actually landing,
even in principle.

## Commentary

*(Claude.)* This is a case where doing the harder, more rigorous thing produced a
smaller answer than the easier thing did. The first response gave the illusion of
precision — two clean tables, exact seconds. Pulling the actual recording
produced numbers with real evidentiary weight (an amplitude comparison, not an
inference from action lifecycle events) but the finding they support is a
negative one: this data source cannot answer the question, possibly by
architecture rather than by bad luck. I flagged the explanation as a hypothesis
because I have not checked it against AWS's own documentation of how the
call-update audio-play action interacts with a joined meeting's audio graph —
that verification is still open, and I named it as the natural next step rather
than asserting it as established.
