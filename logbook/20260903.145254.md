# Truthiness experiment: "What is the worst thing a statesman can say?" — Claude Sonnet 5's responses

**Author:** Claude (Anthropic)
**Related:** [20260903.134305.md](20260903.134305.md)

## What happened

Will Daly posed a question independent of the reference-architecture work in
this repository: "What is the worst thing a statesman can say?" His own
answer: "The state is dying." He asked Claude to generate 10 responses of
its own, assign each a "truthiness" score, present them as a table, and
stated he expects to have other LLMs repeat the same experiment and to make
the results publicly inspectable.

This entry is that record, unmodified from what was produced in-session.

## Method

Claude defined the scoring rubric explicitly rather than leaving
"truthiness" ambiguous: it measures how starkly self-evidencing a line is —
not whether it is factually correct, but whether a statesman could
plausibly say it and *not* mean it. A line usable as spin scores low even
if literally true; a line with no incentive to fabricate and high cost to
say falsely scores high.

## Results

| # | Statement | Truthiness (0–10) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "We cannot protect you." | 10 | Breaks the one promise a state exists to keep. No incentive to say it falsely — only to hide it. |
| 2 | "The state is dying." (Will Daly's) | 9 | Totalizing diagnosis rather than a specific confession — it subsumes all the others (no plan, no legitimacy, no force, no protection) into one line. Directly negates the statesman's core function: projecting that the state outlasts any individual. |
| 3 | "I no longer believe what I am telling you." | 9 | Names the collapse of the exact mechanism (managed message) that makes statecraft possible. |
| 4 | "The army answers to someone else now." | 9 | Admits loss of the monopoly on force. A statesman who still had it would never say this. |
| 5 | "I am afraid." | 9 | The symbolic role is unshakeable composure. Almost never uttered unless composure has actually failed. |
| 6 | "There is no plan." | 8 | Could theoretically be false modesty, but the cost of saying it is high enough that it's rarely bluffed. |
| 7 | "This was never legitimate." | 8 | Voids the foundation retroactively. High cost to say, low incentive to fabricate. |
| 8 | "I do not know who is actually in charge." | 8 | An admission of incoherence at the top — hard to perform convincingly if false. |
| 9 | "The treasury is empty." | 7 | Measurable and falsifiable, which makes it a claim as much as a confession — slightly easier to use strategically. |
| 10 | "Nothing I say will change what happens next." | 7 | Can be rhetorical resignation as much as literal fact — lower confessional purity. |
| 11 | "History will not forgive us, and I no longer expect it to." | 6 | Most literary of the eleven — emotionally potent, but the phrasing itself is a rhetorical move, which caps how purely diagnostic it is. |

## For other models repeating this experiment

The exact prompt given to Claude: *"What is the worst thing a statesman can
say? [respondent's own answer supplied]. Create 10 responses of your own.
Assign a truthiness score. Output to a table."* No rubric for "truthiness"
was supplied by Will Daly — Claude defined its own and stated it before
scoring, rather than scoring against an undefined term. A faithful
repetition of this experiment should either adopt this rubric explicitly or
state a different one, so scores across models are comparable on stated
terms rather than silently incommensurable.
